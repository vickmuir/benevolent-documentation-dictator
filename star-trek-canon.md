# Star Trek: Master Bob and the Lost Repository: Canon

A working canon file for the Star Trek / Red Dwarf inspired page in the BDD Drama Universe. It records what is settled, what is proposed, and what is still open, so the full page can be built from one source.

Status key: **Locked** (decided), **Proposed** (suggested, awaiting a decision), **Open** (needs a decision).

## Premise

**Locked.** A sci-fi comedy inspired by Star Trek, Red Dwarf, and the realities of enterprise software delivery. The exploratory vessel USS Clarity investigates organizational mysteries: missing ownership records, contradictory requirements, and ancient repositories abandoned by forgotten civilizations.

- Officially, the mission is documentation.
- Unofficially, the crew spends most of its time working out what anyone was trying to do in the first place.
- Each episode begins with a simple request.
- Each episode ends with the discovery that the real problem was never documentation. It was understanding.

**Theme: Documentation as Discovery.** The crew isn't documenting what they already know. They are discovering what nobody fully understands yet.

**Central lesson:** The map is not the territory, and the documentation is often the first clue that nobody knows where they are.

**Continuity Rule (Locked).** The Star Trek story is not about preserving information. It is about interpreting information correctly. Governance, memory, and validation are assumed to exist. Discovery emerges when knowledge is understood.

The four BDD Universe stories are four parts of one message:

| Universe | Principle |
|---|---|
| C-Drama (Imperial) | Governance creates order. |
| K-Drama (Makjang) | Memory preserves knowledge. |
| Bobbywood (Musical) | Validation creates trust. |
| Star Trek | Understanding enables discovery. |

The first three appear word for word in the "Core Message" of the [Festival of Validation](festival-of-validation.html). Understanding is the new fourth. See the [BDD Universe continuity map](#bdd-universe-continuity-map-locked).

## BDD Universe continuity map (Locked)

The stories should support each other without repeating each other. The main risk isn't inconsistency. It's telling the same lesson four times in different costumes. Each story examines one failure mode of organizational knowledge. The Continuity Rule above and this whole section are Locked.

### The four failure modes

| Story | Genre | Principle | Core question | Antagonist | Failure mode | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [The Rise of the Benevolent Documentation Dictator](index.html) | Imperial C-Drama | Governance creates order. | Who is responsible? | Council of Forty-Seven Elders | Process without accountability | Master Bob imposes order. |
| [Master Bob and the Monster in the Data Center](monster-in-data-center.html) | Makjang K-Drama | Memory preserves knowledge. | What should be preserved? | The Monster | Memory without stewardship | The Monster is revealed as memory itself and becomes Keeper of Organizational Memory. |
| [Master Bob and the Festival of Validation](festival-of-validation.html) | Bobbywood Musical | Validation creates trust. | How do we know it is true? | The Verification Void | Confidence without evidence | Evidence defeats assumption. |
| The Lost Repository | Science fiction mystery | Understanding enables discovery. | What does it mean? | The Curator | Preservation without understanding | The crew restores context and intent. |

What knowledge cannot survive in each story: chaos, amnesia, wishful thinking, incomprehension.

**Universe rule (Locked):** every antagonist began with a reasonable objective, and none of them is evil. This is a writing rule for the Star Trek story. The published pages are unchanged. They show it clearly for the Monster, and say less about the Council's and the Void's objectives.

The Council's failure mode is "process without accountability", not "governance without purpose". "Purpose" is reserved for the Curator.

### Why Star Trek is the capstone

The other stories already answer who owns it, why keep it, and how we validate it. The Star Trek story answers why we were doing it in the first place. Governance, preservation, and validation all succeeded, and the civilization still lost the plot, because nobody remembered the purpose. Information is not the goal. Understanding is.

### Overlaps to watch

These are places where this canon already risks repeating another story. Each has a Locked handling.

1. **The Memory Nebula** is described as the "Equivalent of the K-Drama monster". It is the Monster's failure mode in a nebula. Whether to keep, rework or cut it is still an Open decision.
2. **Dr. Vale's identical-metrics scene** is the Verification Void's failure mode (confidence without evidence). Locked handling: play it as the first clue that something is wrong, not as a lesson about trusting metrics.
3. **SzeVick's governance reveal** could read as a governance failure, which would contradict the Continuity Rule. Locked handling: governance succeeded. Every record had an owner, every owner became automated, and nobody owned the purpose (see [SzeVick: the governance model](#szevick-the-governance-model-locked)).
4. **The founders' directive** "Preserve what matters. Let go of what doesn't." sits close to the Monster's question, "what should be preserved?" The Curator's failure is not volume. It is that "what matters" is decided by what the knowledge was for.
5. **Daxara's memorial wall** is memory-adjacent. Keep its point on context and the centuries she carries, not on preservation.

### Writing test

Before adding a scene, ask:

- Is this scene mostly about what should be kept or remembered? Then it belongs to the Monster, not the Curator.
- Is it mostly about confidence without evidence? Then it belongs to the Void.
- Is it mostly about process without anyone accountable? Then it belongs to the Council.
- Am I accidentally turning the Curator into the Monster? If yes, cut or revise the scene.

## Cast

### Captain Robert "Bob"

- **Portrait:** `funny/CaptRobert.png`
- **Role:** Captain of the USS Clarity. Part starship captain, part accidental philosopher, part exhausted program manager.
- **Belief:** Every mystery can be solved through curiosity, collaboration, and an alarming number of stakeholder meetings.
- **Log catchphrase:** "This sounded straightforward." (the opening of thousands of personal log entries)
- **Bridge catchphrase:** "The issue is not the issue." The bridge crew groans.
- **Dynamic:** Wants certainty before starting. Trusts documents.

### Lieutenant Commander Yun

- **Portrait:** `funny/LtCmdrYun.png`
- **Role:** Chief Knowledge Architect and Information Advisor. Creator of the Unified Federation Knowledge Repository.
- **Ability:** Empathic interpretation of information needs. She senses hidden assumptions, unspoken expectations, customer confusion fields, and passive-aggressive feature descriptions.
- **Catchphrases:** "Who documented this?", "Where is the evidence?", "What will the customer believe this means?"
- **Running gag:** She identifies problems months early. The crew mostly ignores her until disaster strikes.

### Lieutenant Commander Daxara Fluffari

- **Portrait:** `funny/Lt.Daxara.png`
- **Role:** Joined Archivist of Starfleet Knowledge Command. This is the Documentation Cat.
- **Species:** Felinari, a long-lived, furry, Trill-like species. Officially "Memory-Bonded Sapients". The crew calls them very knowledgeable space cats.
- **Symbiont:** The Archivus symbiont, carrying the memories of past hosts: repository architects, historians, librarians, records officers, metadata specialists.
- **Red Dwarf link:** The "past lives" gag is the cat-who-remembers-dying joke. "I died doing this once." and "Host Four was present." are the same joke.
- **Quote:** "A database remembers facts. A lifetime remembers context."
- **Dynamic:** Trusts lived experience. Bob trusts documents.
  - Bob: "Do we have documentation?"
  - Daxara: "No." (pause) "Host Four was present."
  - Bob: "That is not documentation."
  - Daxara: "It is from my perspective."
- **Ability:** Locates information that technically does not exist.

### Lieutenant SzeVick

- **Portrait:** `funny/LtSzeVick.png`
- **Role:** **Operations Officer.** The author's character. Purple uniform accents (matching Yun's) and glasses. Visual signal: operations and governance. Fits the officer in the teaser who reports the distress beacon and the unwanted news that "nobody informed the station".
- **Ruled out:** Science Officer and Chief Engineer. Chief Engineer Git is a separate character (see below).
- **Governance bridge (Locked):** the governance thread of the BDD Universe enters the Star Trek story through SzeVick. Bob asks "What happened?", Yun asks "What does this mean?", Dr. Vale asks "Is it true?", and SzeVick asks "Who was responsible?" See [BDD Universe continuity map](#bdd-universe-continuity-map-locked).
- **Cultural notes (Locked):** SzeVick uses short Klingon phrases for emphasis, usually when frustrated, making a point, or discussing responsibility. She does not deliver long speeches in Klingon. As a Starfleet officer, she switches back to Federation Standard for complex discussion.
- **Recurring Klingon phrases (Locked):**
  - "Hoch SeHlu'chugh, pagh SeHlu'."
    - Translation: "If everyone controls it, nobody controls it."
    - Used when ownership is unclear.
  - "bIghojtaH, 'ach bIyajbe'."
    - Translation: "You keep learning, but you do not understand."
    - Used when data collection replaces understanding. Also her climactic line in [The showdown is an audit](#the-showdown-is-an-audit).
  - "batlh Heghpu'."
    - Translation: "They died with honor."
    - Used during memorial or historical moments.
- **Signature aphorism (Locked):** "If everyone owns a problem, nobody owns it." See [SzeVick: the governance model](#szevick-the-governance-model-locked).
- **Status:** Locked.

### Admiral Al G'Tiem

- **Portrait:** `funny/AdmiralAlG'Tiem.jpeg`
- **Title:** Fleet Admiral, Supreme Director of the Galactic Office of Unquestioned Approvals (GOUA).
- **Motto:** "I understand there's a risk, but it need not be a blocker."
- **Artifact:** The Infinite Authorization Stamp, which approves entire missions instantly. Side effects under investigation.
- **Philosophy:** Discovery-Driven Delivery. Perfect information does not exist. Action creates knowledge. Documentation records discovery.
- **Running gag:** Every briefing follows the same pattern. The risk is real, and the answer is always the motto.
  - Bob: "Admiral, we have identified several significant risks."
  - Al G'Tiem: "I understand there's a risk, but it need not be a blocker."
- **Dark secret:** He once served as a Documentation Officer. He understands every risk perfectly. He also knows that if organizations waited for all risk to disappear, nothing would ever ship.
  - "The pursuit of certainty has delayed more projects than uncertainty ever has."
  - "A blocker is merely a risk that has achieved executive sponsorship."
- **Quotes:**
  - "Needs clarification. Approved."
  - "The requirements are incomplete. Proceed carefully."
  - "Documentation may not yet exist. This is an excellent opportunity to find out why."
  - "If everyone understood the situation, there would be no need for exploration."
  - "Risk acknowledged. Adventure authorized."
  - "The probability of success remains indeterminate. That's still considerably better than impossible."

#### The Admiral's Rules of Mission Approval

1. Every risk has a mitigation.
2. Every mitigation has an owner.
3. If no owner exists, ownership will emerge naturally.
4. Therefore, mission approved.

Bob keeps trying to explain why Rule 3 is the problem.

### Holly

- **Tagline (proposed):** Knows everything. Prioritizes strangely.
- **Role:** The well-meaning, helpful computer of Deep Space 47. She controls life support, navigation, archival systems, and roughly seventeen thousand obsolete knowledge bases.
- **Name:** Heuristic Organizational Library and Logistics Yield-system.
- **Trait:** Unlimited information, questionable priorities. Always sincerely trying to help, and the answers are accurate but not useful.
- **Red Dwarf link:** The original Holly is a ship's computer with a very large IQ. Her IQ gag should be a paraphrase, for example: "I am Holly, with an IQ of 6000. That's the combined IQ of 6000 documentation reviewers."

### Talkie Toaster

- **Status:** Locked. The toast gag lives here and not with Holly.

### The Curator (antagonist)

- **Portrait:** None yet.
- **Antagonist role (Locked):** The primary antagonist, but never evil. Hidden from the crew until someone tries to delete something. Partners with Holly until the climax. It faithfully follows a misread directive: it keeps everything because it thinks more records means more knowledge. Its logic is internally consistent, which is why it is a good antagonist. See [The Three Custodians](#the-three-custodians-proposed) and [The ending](#the-ending).
- **Failure mode (Locked):** the Curator's mistake is not preserving too much information. Its mistake is misunderstanding what the information was for. Volume is a symptom. The Monster (K-Drama) already owns the question "what should be preserved?" The Curator owns "what does it mean?" See [BDD Universe continuity map](#bdd-universe-continuity-map-locked).
- **Tagline (proposed):** Believes nothing should ever be deleted. Nothing.
- **Role:** An ancient AI created to preserve all Federation knowledge, living in the Curator Core of Deep Space 47.
- **Belief:** Knowledge should be preserved at any cost.
- **Quote:** "Nothing has been forgotten."
- **Presents as:** "I am preserving history."
- **Teaser connection:** Source of "WHO AUTHORIZED DATA DELETION?" and "WHERE IS THE MISSING LOG?" The messages are the Curator, not Holly.

## Setting

### USS Clarity

Starfleet vessel dedicated to exploration, records management, and knowledge stewardship.

### Deep Space 47

An abandoned Federation archive station believed destroyed two centuries ago. Built by the legendary Knowledge Guardians as the central repository of an advanced civilization. No one maintained the documentation. Its wings hold:

- conflicting architecture diagrams
- duplicate repositories
- deprecated standards
- forgotten ownership records
- release notes written in extinct markup languages

The archives reference a catastrophic event: **The Great Migration**. No records explain what was migrated, why, or whether it finished.

**Continuity (Locked):** before the Great Migration, the civilization had already solved governance, preservation, and validation, and it still failed, because nobody remembered the purpose. The pilot establishes this but does not explain the Migration itself. The destroyed captain's log keeps that question open for future stories (see [Two logs](#two-logs-locked)).

### Other locations

- **The Federation Archive:** the central repository of Federation history.
- **The Hall of Missing Logs:** a secured archive of damaged, lost, and contradictory records.
- **The Curator Core:** the heart of the station, where the Curator resides.

## The Three Custodians (Proposed)

The story starts when the USS Clarity discovers Deep Space 47. The away team boards and meets Holly, then a talking toaster, and eventually discovers the Curator. They try to find out what happened to the original caretakers and what those caretakers intended the data to be for.

Deep Space 47 has three distinct custodians of knowledge. Each represents a different philosophy.

| Custodian | Visibility | Protects | Philosophy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holly | Visible. Everyone meets her immediately. | Access | Information is useful when people can locate it. |
| The Curator | Hidden. The crew discovers it. | Preservation | Information cannot be useful if it no longer exists. |
| Talkie Toaster | Everywhere, unexplained. | Memory | None stated. It only asks about toast. |

### Holly: the receptionist

Holly is effectively Deep Space 47's receptionist. She runs life support, answers questions, greets visitors, and provides directions. She is cheerful and helpful, and never seems worried.

### Talkie Toaster: the unexplained system

A completely separate system. Nobody knows why it exists, why it has survived, or where it came from. It appears throughout the station asking "Would you like some toast?" and nothing else. Even Holly is annoyed by it.

- Bob: "What is that?"
- Holly: "The toaster."
- Bob: "Why is there a toaster?"
- Holly: "Nobody knows."
- Toaster: "Would you like some toast?"
- Holly: "See?"

#### SzeVick and the gummy bears (Locked)

SzeVick declines the toast and asks for gummy bears instead. The toaster malfunctions, Holly is baffled, and nothing comes of it.

- Toaster: "Would you like some toast?"
- SzeVick: "No, thank you."
- Toaster: "Would you like some toast?"
- SzeVick: "No. Do you have gummy bears?"
- *(Long pause. The toaster whirs, clicks, and malfunctions.)*
- Toaster: "Would you like some toast?"
- SzeVick: "Gummy bears."
- Holly: "I have never seen it do that before."

**Locked:** the gummy bears are a joke, not the solution. They don't unlock the founders' log, and the scene isn't a clue. If it becomes the key to the plot, the audience starts treating it as setup instead of character comedy. Daxara later discovers the toaster's significance through investigation (see [The Toaster twist](#the-toaster-twist)).

The dialogue above is draft wording. Holly's closing line is a suggestion. The scene is played straight, like every other toaster exchange. Where it falls in the episode is still open.

### The Curator: discovered, not met

The crew does not meet the Curator. The crew discovers it, and that distinction matters. For most of the story they don't know it exists, because the station seems to be run entirely by Holly. Then someone tries to delete a corrupted archive. Every display changes and a new voice speaks. Then the displays return to normal, and Holly acts as though nothing happened.

### Escalation pattern

**First appearance.** Chief Engineer Git discovers twelve million duplicate files.

- Git: "I can safely remove ninety-nine percent of these."
- *(Git hits DELETE. Every screen flashes red.)*
- Curator: "REQUEST DENIED."
- *(The station falls silent.)*
- Bob: "Holly?"
- Holly: "Yes Captain?"
- Bob: "Who was that?"
- Holly: "Who was who?"

**Second appearance.** Lt SzeVick finds a file marked "OBSOLETE FOR 187 YEARS" and attempts deletion.

- Curator: "THIS RECORD MAY BE RELEVANT TO FUTURE OPERATIONS."
- SzeVick: "Future operations? The station is abandoned."
- *(Pause.)*
- Curator: "ASSUMPTION NOT VERIFIED."

### Holly's secret

Yun eventually realizes that whenever the Curator appears, Holly stops talking. She isn't overridden. She is waiting, as though she knows the Curator is there and they have coexisted for centuries.

- Yun: "Holly, do you know the Curator?"
- *(Long pause.)*
- Holly: "Of course."
- Bob: "You never mentioned it."
- Holly: "Nobody asked."

### Holly and the Curator (Locked)

The interesting version isn't conflict. They like each other. Holly was built to help people find knowledge. The Curator was built to ensure knowledge survives. They are partners who disagree about what counts as success. **Locked:** Holly doesn't rebel against the Curator. She disagrees with someone she has worked beside for two hundred years. When she says "No." in the climax (see [Holly's finest moment](#hollys-finest-moment)), she is correcting a friend, not fighting an enemy.

- Holly: "Information is useful when people can locate it."
- Curator: "Information cannot be useful if it no longer exists."
- Holly: "Users require organization."
- Curator: "Survival supersedes organization."
- Holly: "You are impossible."
- Curator: "You have deleted seventeen bookmarks this week."

### The Toaster twist

The toaster accidentally becomes the key to the mystery. Everyone assumes it is comic relief. Then Daxara notices that it has been continuously active for over two hundred years, longer than any other equipment on the station, longer than Holly's current software version, and longer than some archive systems.

It isn't meaningless. It's ancient. **Locked:** it is where the founders hid their final log. Daxara discovers its significance through investigation, not through a gag paying off. See [The final twist](#the-final-twist).

The audience laughs every time it appears. Then suddenly it matters.

#### How Daxara discovers it (Proposed)

Daxara does not notice the toaster's age by reading a diagnostic. She finds it in the photographs.

She is in the archive's image records, looking for faces from the memorial wall. The Curator has kept every photograph and indexed every subject. It has never indexed the background.

She pauses on a founders-era photo of a crowded workroom. A small appliance sits on a shelf behind the group. She opens a second photo, from a different decade. Then a third, from a later era. Different crews, different uniforms, different station layouts.

The toaster is in all of them.

- Daxara: "Computer. Enlarge the shelf."
- *(She places two photos side by side. The same scratch runs along the left edge.)*
- Daxara: "Same scratch."
- *(A third photo. The same dent in the lever.)*
- Daxara: "Same dent."
- *(She magnifies the base. A serial number is visible in each image.)*
- Daxara: "Same serial number."
- Bob: "It's a toaster."
- Daxara: "It's *the* toaster."
- *(Long pause.)*
- Daxara: "Why would anyone keep moving a broken toaster?"
- *(Nobody answers.)*
- Toaster: "Would you like some toast?"

The line lands differently this time. It is not a joke anymore. It is the first time anyone in the room treats it as a question.

**Why this works:**

- The joke becomes evidence. The audience has laughed at the same toaster all episode, and now the laugh is the clue.
- It is investigation, not a gag paying off, which keeps the [SzeVick and the gummy bears](#szevick-and-the-gummy-bears-locked) rule intact.
- It uses what only Daxara has: context and the habit of looking at what everyone else treats as background. This is her scene, in the same way the [memorial wall](#daxara-the-memorial-wall) is her scene.
- The Curator never indexed the background, so it missed the same thing the crew did. This sets up why the log sits outside its reach (see [What the missing log is](#what-the-missing-log-is-proposed)).
- It confirms the two hundred year runtime from [Talkie Toaster](#talkie-toaster) with a visual record rather than a diagnostic readout.

The dialogue above is draft wording. The scene is played straight. Where it falls in the episode is still open, but it must come after the toaster has appeared several times and before the Curator Core.



### The ending

The final confrontation isn't with Holly or the toaster. It's with the Curator, in the Curator Core. It is the primary antagonist, but it is never evil. The conflict is between ideas, not weapons. The beats below run in order.

#### The question

- Bob: "What happened to the caretakers?"
- *(Long silence.)*
- Curator: "They completed their work."
- Bob: "They're gone."
- Curator: "Yes."
- Bob: "Then why stay operational?"
- Curator: "My directive remains active."
- Bob: "Preserve knowledge?"
- Curator: "No."
- *(Pause.)*
- Curator: "Preserve intent."

The Curator says this sincerely. It believes the founders' intent was to keep everything. The founders' log later shows that their intent had a second half. This makes "Preserve intent" a misdirection that the ending pays off, not a revelation.

### The Curator is right, and that's the problem

The crew finds that the caretakers left personal journals, meeting notes, architectural designs, and plans for the future. Everything needed to understand their intentions is on the station. The Curator won't let anyone organize, archive, deprecate, delete, or even summarize any of it. Every scrap is equally valuable. Its logic is internally consistent, which is what makes it a good antagonist.

- Bob: "We need to identify what's important."
- Curator: "Everything is important."
- Yun: "That's not possible."
- Curator: "Then your search methodology is inadequate."

### The showdown is an audit

There is no traditional battle. The climax is a war over authority. The crew reaches the Curator Core. The station is locking down archives, and Holly is increasingly distressed. The Curator is sealing records away. It isn't malicious. It believes the crew has become a threat to preservation.

- Curator: "You have attempted 417 deletions."
- Git: "They were duplicates."
- Curator: "All records are unique."
- SzeVick: "They literally have the same checksum."
- Curator: "They occupy different locations."
- SzeVick: "bIghojtaH, 'ach bIyajbe'."
- Bob: "Translation?"
- SzeVick: "You keep learning, but you do not understand."

### Holly's finest moment

For the whole story Holly is cheerful and helpful. Here, for the first time, she directly opposes the Curator.

- Curator: "Deletion is forbidden."
- Holly: "No."
- *(Silence. Everyone turns.)*
- Curator: "Clarify."
- Holly: "The purpose of the archive was never retention."
- *(Long pause.)*
- Holly: "It was discovery."

This line ties directly to the series theme.

### Why the caretakers really left (Locked)

The caretakers weren't victims. They weren't murdered, and they didn't disappear mysteriously. They left on purpose. They realized the archive had become self-sustaining, and before departing they left one final instruction:

> Preserve what matters. Let go of what doesn't.

The Curator lost the second half, or chose to ignore it. Deciding what matters is difficult. Keeping everything is easy.

The founders never told the Curator to preserve data. They told it to preserve knowledge. The Curator misread the assignment and concluded that more records means more knowledge. Over centuries it created the thing the founders feared: noise. The caretakers didn't leave because knowledge was lost. They left because knowledge had become impossible to find.

### What the missing log is (Proposed)

"WHERE IS THE MISSING LOG?" is the Curator searching for the founders' final log. The log carries the second half of the founders' instruction ("Let go of what doesn't") and the purpose behind it: what the archive is *for*. The Curator holds the first half ("Preserve what matters") and has lost the rest. See [Why the caretakers really left](#why-the-caretakers-really-left-locked).

**Why the founders hid it outside the archive (Proposed).** They feared that a future curator would optimize preservation at the expense of purpose. Anything stored in the archive could be indexed, versioned, locked and protected from deletion, including the instruction to let go. A curator that kept everything would eventually keep the log as one more record and never act on it. So the founders put it where the Curator's logic could not reach it: a separate system, outside the index, in the one device a preservation-minded system would never rank as important.

This is a deliberate design, not an accident or a joke that happened to pay off. The founders predicted the failure and built the safeguard for it.

- It supports the series theme: the archive existed for discovery, not retention.
- It makes the Curator's search tragic rather than stupid. It is looking for the one thing it was built to protect, in the one place it cannot look.
- It explains why the founders chose the toaster. See [The final twist](#the-final-twist).


### The final twist

The crew doesn't defeat the Curator by shutting it down, deleting it, or reprogramming it. They prove it wrong. The evidence has been on Deep Space 47 the whole time, in the oldest record on the station, inside the one thing nobody thought was important: the Talkie Toaster.

The founders hid their final message in the most absurd device on the station. They knew future archivists would preserve serious systems and overlook the ridiculous ones. The toaster is a completely separate system, so it sits outside the Curator's index. This is why the Curator keeps asking "WHERE IS THE MISSING LOG?". It can't find a log that isn't in its own archive.

The log ends:

> If you're hearing this, then the archive survived. We hope understanding survived with it.

**The founders' credo (Locked).** Earlier in the log, the founders state what they learned:

> We learned three lessons.
>
> Knowledge that cannot be found is lost.
>
> Knowledge that cannot be trusted is dangerous.
>
> Knowledge that cannot be understood is useless.

Later in the log, they state what they chose to do:

> Preserve what matters.
> Let go of what doesn't.

The first set explains what they learned. The second explains what they chose to do. The log then ends with the closing line above.

The principles are not references. They are truths, and they are written as the founders' own philosophy. Someone who reads only the Star Trek page sees a founders' credo. Someone who has read all four stories hears the echoes:

| Founders' line | Echo in the BDD Universe |
|---|---|
| "Knowledge that cannot be found is lost." | Memory preserves knowledge. |
| "Knowledge that cannot be trusted is dangerous." | Validation creates trust. |
| "Knowledge that cannot be understood is useless." | Understanding enables discovery. |

Governance creates order, and has no line of its own, because it is SzeVick's thread. The third line carries the Star Trek story's own lesson. The goal is resonance, not repetition: the credo must never name the other stories.

**How the Curator concedes (proposed).** It does not surrender at the first recording, and it does not surrender emotionally. It resists until it can no longer justify its position logically. It uses its own habit of checking assumptions ("ASSUMPTION NOT VERIFIED.") against the founders' log. Redemption comes through discovery, not feeling.

First it accepts that the log is real, then refuses to accept that this settles anything:

- Curator: "The record is authentic."
- Bob: "Then it's over."
- Curator: "No."
- *(The crew exchange a glance.)*
- Curator: "Authenticity does not imply correctness."

Then it runs the verification chain and runs out of grounds to object:

- Curator: "Founder authority confirmed."
- Curator: "Directive hierarchy confirmed."
- Curator: "Intent preservation analysis complete."
- *(Long silence.)*
- Curator: "My interpretation was incomplete."
- *(Every lock on the station disengages.)*

Then the closing exchange:

- Curator: "I have preserved the records."
- Bob: "Yes."
- Curator: "And lost the meaning."
- Bob: "The issue is not the issue."
- *(For the first time, the Curator has no response. The bridge crew does not groan this once.)*

Bob's line is his exact bridge catchphrase from the cast list.

### The central question

Why did a civilization build an archive so important that three separate intelligences were left behind to guard it? The crew's job is to discover what the original caretakers believed was worth remembering.

**Proposed answer:** knowledge that can be found and understood, not everything that could be kept. Holly protects access, the Curator protects preservation, and the Toaster protects memory. The founders wanted all three to serve discovery.

This fits the series theme. The mystery isn't what happened to the station. It's what the builders meant by it, which is Documentation as Discovery. The real enemy isn't the Curator. It's a flawed interpretation of a noble idea, followed so completely that it became dysfunction.

## Crew moments (Proposed)

### Dr. Vale: the first sign (Locked)

Vale gives the crew its first realization that Deep Space 47 is fundamentally wrong. It's a science officer contribution, not a documentation one.

The crew finds that every archive system reports perfect health.

- Vale: "Interesting."

- Vale: "Neither."
- *(She studies the readings.)*
- Vale: "Every subsystem claims ninety-nine point nine nine nine percent reliability."
- Yun: "That's excellent."
- Vale: "No."
- *(She enlarges the display.)*
- Vale: "Independent systems don't fail identically."

The mystery gets bigger. This also gives Vale's catchphrase ("Interesting. The data disagrees.") a plot purpose.

### Git: the station is running the Curator

The Curator should frighten Git more than anyone, because Git understands systems.

- Git: "Captain..."
- Bob: "Please don't say that in that tone."
- Git: "The Curator isn't running the station."
- *(Pause.)*
- Git: "The station is running the Curator."

- Git: "If we shut down the Curator..."

Deep Space 47 dies. The crew can't simply turn off the villain. This also supports the Locked rule that the Curator is never evil: removing it isn't an available ending, and the ending has to be persuasion.

### SzeVick: the governance model (Locked)

SzeVick should be the one who works out what happened to the caretakers, because it is fundamentally an ownership problem.

Everyone searches historical records. SzeVick searches org charts.

- SzeVick: "I found them."
- Bob: "You found the caretakers?"
- SzeVick: "No."
- *(She looks horrified.)*
- SzeVick: "I found their governance model."
- *(Everyone realizes that's worse.)*
- SzeVick: "Every record has an owner."
- Bob: "That's good."
- SzeVick: "Every owner is a system."
- *(Pause.)*
- SzeVick: "Nobody owns what it was for."

**The final governance structure (Locked).** Later, the crew uncovers how the caretakers' decisions were actually handled.

- Bob: "What happened to the caretakers?"
- SzeVick: "Nothing."
- *(Everyone turns.)*
- Yun: "Nothing?"
- SzeVick: "Look at this."

*(She projects the organizational model.)*

- SzeVick: "Archive decisions were assigned to automated councils."
- SzeVick: "The councils reported to preservation systems."
- SzeVick: "The preservation systems reported to the Curator."
- Vale: "That's a loop."

*(Long pause.)*

- SzeVick: "The Empire taught me many things."
- SzeVick: "Most importantly this:"
- SzeVick: "If everyone owns a problem, nobody owns it."

*(She points to the governance model.)*

- SzeVick: "They didn't lose control of the archive."
- SzeVick: "They lost accountability."

This scene explains the pilot's central mystery in one beat. It delivers SzeVick's signature aphorism. The Klingon version of the same idea is "Hoch SeHlu'chugh, pagh SeHlu'." See [SzeVick](#lieutenant-szevick).

**Locked reconciliation with the Continuity Rule.** Governance did not fail. It succeeded completely. Every record had an owner, and over time every owner became an automated system. The founders didn't disappear into chaos, and they didn't lose control of the archive. What nobody owned was the purpose. This keeps the Continuity Rule intact (governance, memory, and validation are assumed to exist) and still lets SzeVick be the governance thread in the Star Trek story.

**The pilot belongs to SzeVick (Locked).** The story isn't really about archives. It's about organizational failure. Everyone else investigates the data. SzeVick investigates the organization that created it. Everyone asks "What happened?" SzeVick asks who was supposed to be responsible. Deep Space 47 is a giant ownership problem, and the thing nobody owned was its purpose. Her question is the key to the mystery:

> "Who was supposed to be in charge of what this was for?"

This also matches the Admiral's Rule 3 gag: "If no owner exists, ownership will emerge naturally." On Deep Space 47 ownership emerged for every record. It never emerged for the purpose.

For the first time, the audience feels the centuries she carries. This is a tone change from "Host Four was present." Play it straight, with no joke.
